\section{The assertion}

Wolf's Kramers theorem~II concerns a Hermitian matrix $H$ and a nonzero
anti-symmetric matrix $A$ satisfying an intertwining relation. It asserts that
\begin{align}
    H&=H^\dagger,
    &
    A^T&=-A,
    &
    A&\neq 0,
    &
    HA&=AH^T
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    \dim\ker(H-\lambda\Id)\geq 2
    \ \text{for every eigenvalue $\lambda$ of $H$}.
    \label{eq:wolf_ch3_kramers_theorem_ii_printed_claim}
\end{align}
This is the statement printed as Chapter~3, Theorem~3.2, ``Kramer's
theorem~II'', in Ref.~\cite{Wolf2012Quantum}.\footnote{Local source:
\path{Notes/WolfNoteTexSource/ch03_positive_not_completely.tex},
lines~527--530. Tracked in \ghissue{17}.}

The theorem is presented as a relaxation of Wolf's first Kramers theorem
\cite[Chapter~3, Theorem~3.1]{Wolf2012Quantum}. That theorem assumes that a
Hermitian matrix $H$ commutes with an anti-unitary operator $T$ satisfying
$T^2=-\Id$. Writing $T=\Gamma V$, where $\Gamma$ is complex conjugation and
$V$ is unitary, gives
\begin{align}
    HV^\dagger&=V^\dagger H^T,
    &
    V^T&=-V.
    \label{eq:wolf_ch3_kramers_theorem_ii_first_theorem_matrix_form}
\end{align}
Wolf then states that ``the restriction on the required symmetry in the
theorem can be relaxed as the same proof shows''
\cite[Chapter~3, Theorems~3.1--3.2]{Wolf2012Quantum}.\footnote{Local source:
lines~498--501 for the matrix reduction and lines~523--525 for the relaxation
claim.}

\section{The point at issue}

Let $\ket{\psi}\neq 0$ satisfy
\begin{align}
    H\ket{\psi}&=\lambda\ket{\psi}.
    \label{eq:wolf_ch3_kramers_theorem_ii_eigenvector}
\end{align}
Because $H$ is Hermitian, $\lambda$ is real. Complex conjugation of
Eq.~\ref{eq:wolf_ch3_kramers_theorem_ii_eigenvector} therefore gives
\begin{align}
    H^T\ket{\overline{\psi}}
        &=\overline{H\ket{\psi}}
    \label{eq:wolf_ch3_kramers_theorem_ii_conjugate_eigenvector_first}\\
        &=\lambda\ket{\overline{\psi}}.
    \label{eq:wolf_ch3_kramers_theorem_ii_conjugate_eigenvector_second}
\end{align}
The intertwining relation then yields
\begin{align}
    HA\ket{\overline{\psi}}
        &=AH^T\ket{\overline{\psi}}
    \label{eq:wolf_ch3_kramers_theorem_ii_partner_transport_first}\\
        &=\lambda A\ket{\overline{\psi}}.
    \label{eq:wolf_ch3_kramers_theorem_ii_partner_transport_second}
\end{align}
Thus $A\ket{\overline{\psi}}$ is either zero or an eigenvector of $H$ with
eigenvalue $\lambda$.

Anti-symmetry also gives
\begin{align}
    \braket{\psi}{A\overline{\psi}}
        &=\overline{\psi}^{\,T}A\overline{\psi}
    \label{eq:wolf_ch3_kramers_theorem_ii_partner_orthogonality_first}\\
        &=\overline{\psi}^{\,T}A^T\overline{\psi}
    \label{eq:wolf_ch3_kramers_theorem_ii_partner_orthogonality_second}\\
        &=-\overline{\psi}^{\,T}A\overline{\psi}
    \label{eq:wolf_ch3_kramers_theorem_ii_partner_orthogonality_third}\\
        &=0.
    \label{eq:wolf_ch3_kramers_theorem_ii_partner_orthogonality_zero}
\end{align}
The second equality uses the fact that a scalar equals its transpose.
Equations~\ref{eq:wolf_ch3_kramers_theorem_ii_partner_transport_second}
and~\ref{eq:wolf_ch3_kramers_theorem_ii_partner_orthogonality_zero} establish
the transport and orthogonality steps in the printed proof. They do not
establish that the partner is nonzero. In the first Kramers theorem, the
partner is $V^\dagger\ket{\overline{\psi}}$, which cannot vanish because $V$
is unitary. A general nonzero anti-symmetric matrix $A$ may have a nontrivial
kernel.

A counterexample occurs on $\C^3$. Define
\begin{align}
    H&=\operatorname{diag}(0,1,1),
    &
    A&=
    \begin{pmatrix}
        0&0&0\\
        0&0&1\\
        0&-1&0
    \end{pmatrix}.
    \label{eq:wolf_ch3_kramers_theorem_ii_counterexample_matrices}
\end{align}
These matrices satisfy
\begin{align}
    H&=H^\dagger,
    &
    A^T&=-A,
    &
    A&\neq 0,
    \label{eq:wolf_ch3_kramers_theorem_ii_counterexample_basic_hypotheses}\\
    HA&=AH^T=A.
    \label{eq:wolf_ch3_kramers_theorem_ii_counterexample_intertwining}
\end{align}
The nonzero rows and columns of $A$ lie in the $1$-eigenspace of $H$, which
explains Eq.~\ref{eq:wolf_ch3_kramers_theorem_ii_counterexample_intertwining}.
The eigenvalue $0$ is simple, with eigenvector $e_1$, and its proposed partner
vanishes:
\begin{align}
    He_1&=0,
    &
    \dim\ker H&=1,
    &
    A\overline{e_1}&=Ae_1=0.
    \label{eq:wolf_ch3_kramers_theorem_ii_counterexample_simple_eigenvalue}
\end{align}
Thus the hypotheses of
Eq.~\ref{eq:wolf_ch3_kramers_theorem_ii_printed_claim} hold while its
conclusion fails.

\section{The corrected statement}

For an eigenvector $\ket{\psi}$ with eigenvalue $\lambda$, the calculation
proves the exact conditional statement
\begin{align}
    A\ket{\overline{\psi}}\neq 0
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    \dim\ker(H-\lambda\Id)\geq 2.
    \label{eq:wolf_ch3_kramers_theorem_ii_conditional_correction}
\end{align}
Under the hypothesis in
Eq.~\ref{eq:wolf_ch3_kramers_theorem_ii_conditional_correction},
Eqs.~\ref{eq:wolf_ch3_kramers_theorem_ii_partner_transport_second}
and~\ref{eq:wolf_ch3_kramers_theorem_ii_partner_orthogonality_zero} provide
two nonzero orthogonal eigenvectors with eigenvalue $\lambda$.

A global sufficient condition is injectivity of $A$. More precisely,
\begin{align}
    H&=H^\dagger,
    &
    A^T&=-A,
    &
    HA&=AH^T,
    &
    \ker A&=\{0\},
    \label{eq:wolf_ch3_kramers_theorem_ii_invertible_hypotheses}\\
    \dim\ker(H-\lambda\Id)&\geq 2
    \qquad\text{for every eigenvalue $\lambda$ of $H$}.
    \label{eq:wolf_ch3_kramers_theorem_ii_invertible_conclusion}
\end{align}
Here the first line implies the second. In finite dimensions, $\ker A=\{0\}$
is equivalent to invertibility.

If $A$ is a $d\times d$ anti-symmetric matrix, then
\begin{align}
    \det A
        &=\det A^T
    \label{eq:wolf_ch3_kramers_theorem_ii_determinant_transpose}\\
        &=\det(-A)
    \label{eq:wolf_ch3_kramers_theorem_ii_determinant_antisymmetry}\\
        &=(-1)^d\det A.
    \label{eq:wolf_ch3_kramers_theorem_ii_determinant_parity}
\end{align}
Consequently, an invertible anti-symmetric matrix can exist only in even
dimension. This agrees with Wolf's observation that anti-symmetric unitaries
exist only in even dimensions \cite[Chapter~3, discussion following
Theorem~3.1]{Wolf2012Quantum}.\footnote{Local source, lines~523--524.}

The first Kramers theorem is the invertible case of
Eqs.~\ref{eq:wolf_ch3_kramers_theorem_ii_invertible_hypotheses}--%
\ref{eq:wolf_ch3_kramers_theorem_ii_invertible_conclusion}. For
$T=\Gamma V$, set $A=V^\dagger$. The relation $V^T=-V$ and unitarity imply
$A^T=-A$, while unitarity also makes $A$ invertible. The corrected theorem~II
therefore specializes to theorem~I using Wolf's proof
\cite[Chapter~3, Theorems~3.1--3.2]{Wolf2012Quantum}.

\section{Conclusion}

Kramers theorem~II is false as printed in Ref.~\cite{Wolf2012Quantum}. The
condition $A\neq 0$ does not prevent the partner
$A\ket{\overline{\psi}}$ from vanishing. The exact local conclusion is
Eq.~\ref{eq:wolf_ch3_kramers_theorem_ii_conditional_correction}, and the global
degeneracy conclusion follows under the invertibility condition in
Eq.~\ref{eq:wolf_ch3_kramers_theorem_ii_invertible_hypotheses}. These corrected
statements resolve the source error.
